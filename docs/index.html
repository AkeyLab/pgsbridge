Population-genetic and environmental determinants of polygenic score transferability

Kaiqian Zhang, John D. Storey, Joshua M. Akey · Lewis-Sigler Institute for Integrative Genomics, Princeton University
Code, simulations and the source of this page: github.com/AkeyLab/pgsbridge

Polygenic scores lose accuracy when they are carried across populations. The question we set out to answer is which part of that loss is unavoidable and which part is recoverable through better data and better methods. We answer it by removing every practical difficulty at once. Suppose every causal variant and every effect size were known exactly, were the same in both populations, and there were no linkage disequilibrium. What transferability would remain?

What we find is that a population has a ceiling on how well any genotype-based predictor can do, and that the ceiling belongs to the population rather than to the predictor. Transferability is simply the ratio of two such ceilings.

Both of those statements can be made exact, and the two expressions below are the whole of the theory. The first says that a population's ceiling is fixed by just three quantities: the additive genetic variance , meaning how much of the trait is heritable; the average self-kinship , which summarises relatedness and structure within the sample; and the non-genetic variance , which is everything about the trait that no genotype can reach. The second says that transferability from a discovery population A to a target population B is the target's ceiling divided by the discovery population's. We take the first expression apart in section 1 and the second in section 2, so there is nothing here you need to absorb all at once.

Written PA for prediction accuracy and T for transferability. Under Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium the average self-kinship is exactly one half, and the first expression reduces to the familiar narrow-sense heritability.

01

The ceiling in one population

Give a predictor perfect knowledge: every causal variant, every effect size, no estimation error at all. It still cannot predict the part of the trait that is not genetic. What is left is a ceiling, and it belongs to the population. Everything else we do rests on this one expression, so it is worth a moment.

The ceiling for this population, evaluated at the values in the panel. Every number moves as you drag.

The phenotypic variance of one individual, split in two

genetic, non-genetic,

Prediction accuracy is the genetic share of the bar. It is the fraction of phenotypic variance a perfect genotype-based predictor removes, and nothing can remove more. The whole bar is the baseline: the error left by a predictor that ignores genotype entirely and gives every individual the population mean. That is the comparison we measure accuracy against, and its expected loss is exactly the total phenotypic variance.

The special case that makes the rest of our paper simple

Self-kinship is the probability that the two alleles an individual carries at a site are identical by descent. Under Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium every individual has self-kinship exactly one half, so the average is one half too, and the ceiling collapses to the narrow-sense heritability.

Why this matters

Nothing in this expression mentions a prediction algorithm. Two populations can differ in how predictable a trait is even when the trait has exactly the same genetic architecture in both, because they differ in allele frequencies, in relatedness structure, and in environmental variation. That is the whole engine of what follows.

02

Transferability is a ratio of two ceilings

Now there are two populations, so from here on the quantities carry a subscript. The population you tuned in section 1 becomes the discovery population A, and a second one, B, is added as the target. Build the optimal predictor in A and apply it in B. Because the predictor is already perfect, the only thing that changes is which population you are measuring in. Transferability is therefore the target's ceiling divided by the discovery population's ceiling, and there is no reason for that ratio to be at most one. The open circles are where each curve meets a transferability of one. That happens where the determinant being varied equals its counterpart in the other population, since the two populations are then equally predictable. The place where the two curves cross each other is a different point and carries no meaning: when only this determinant differs, they cross at the square root of the current transferability, which is one only if the populations already agree. Reading the signs

Raising the additive genetic variance or the average self-kinship in the target population raises transferability, because both raise how much of the target's trait variance is genetic. Raising the non-genetic variance in the target lowers it. Doing any of the three in the discovery population does the opposite, and the curves cross at exactly one wherever the two populations agree.

Why the baseline predictor uses the true mean, not the sample mean

Our simulated individuals are related to one another, so the sample mean of the trait carries a term in the average off-diagonal kinship that the closed form, which concerns a single individual's variance, does not. Comparing against the true population mean removes that mismatch, and averaging the numerator and denominator losses over 50 redraws before dividing removes the variance a ratio of two noisy estimates would otherwise carry. In our coalescent simulations, in sections 6 and 7, the individuals are unrelated, the off-diagonal term is zero, and we use the ordinary sample estimator unchanged.

06

Out of Africa, and a reversed direction

Now we let demographic history set the allele frequencies rather than specifying them ourselves. We simulated African, European and East Asian samples under an Out-of-Africa coalescent model. The individuals within each sample are unrelated, so every population has average self-kinship one half, and transferability is exactly the ratio of heritabilities. Every difference below therefore comes from demography alone.

What empirical studies report

Polygenic scores trained in African-ancestry cohorts transfer comparatively well into non-African populations, and scores trained in European cohorts lose accuracy when applied to African-ancestry individuals. The direction is the opposite of the one above.

The two are not in conflict

They answer different questions. An empirical transferability is shaped at once by linkage disequilibrium, incomplete ascertainment of causal variants, finite training samples, estimation error and possible effect-size heterogeneity. We remove all of them by construction. So the reversal is informative rather than contradictory: allele-frequency differences on their own favour transfer into African populations, which means the factors we left out must not only dominate the empirical pattern but be strong enough to turn it around.

07

Rare variants amplify the asymmetry

If allele-frequency differences drive the pattern, then loading more of the trait onto rare variants ought to widen it, because the Out-of-Africa bottleneck fell hardest on low-frequency alleles. That is the prediction we test here. Each trait carries 1,000 causal variants; the controls set what fraction of them are rare and how much larger a rare variant's effect is than a common one's. The two panels are the two directions of the same pair, which is what makes the asymmetry legible.

One choice sits underneath all of this: what counts as a rare variant. Our paper pools the three samples and calls a variant rare below a frequency of 0.01 in the pool. That is a modelling choice, not a property of the trait, and a variant that is rare in the pool need not be rare in any single sample. The first control lets you replace it with four alternatives. We are not proposing any of them as more realistic than ours; deciding a causal variant's frequency class inside one population is not an obviously biological model. We include them because the choice turns out to matter, and it seemed better to show that than to leave it unasked.

The two panels always share one vertical scale, so the two directions can be read against each other. Whether that scale is fitted to the current setting or pinned across all of them is a control in the panel. Fitting keeps every view readable; pinning shows how much the heritability matters, since the curves span roughly 0.46 to 2.48 at a European heritability of 0.1 but only 0.86 to 1.18 at 0.8. The panel reports that span either way. Shaded bands are 95% confidence intervals over 200 replicates per point, and the dashed rule marks the selected rare-variant fraction.

What the five schemes are telling us

Move through them and one thing is hard to miss: the size of the asymmetry changes, and under one scheme its direction changes. Deciding rare on the European sample roughly doubles the gap. Requiring a variant to fall in the same class in all three samples closes it almost completely. Drawing causal variants at random, with no frequency criterion at all, lands close to where our pooled choice lands, which is reassuring, since most variants in the panel are rare in the pool anyway. Deciding rare on the African sample turns the asymmetry around: transfer from Africa to Europe now exceeds one, and the reverse falls below it.

None of this escapes the equation at the top of this page. In every scheme the number we measure is the one gives from the additive genetic variance the chosen causal variants happen to carry in each sample. Conditioning on a variant being common in one population selects variants that drifted up in that population, so that population carries more of the variance and its ceiling rises. What the ascertainment decides is which population the mechanism favours, not whether the mechanism operates.

We think that is the more useful way to read our Figure 3. The direction of the asymmetry is a statement about how the causal variants were selected, and its magnitude is a statement about how heritable the trait is. Two studies of the same two populations can report asymmetries pointing opposite ways without either being wrong, which is a reason to say plainly how the variants were chosen.
