Population-genetic and environmental determinants of polygenic score transferability

Kaiqian Zhang, John D. Storey, Joshua M. Akey · Lewis-Sigler Institute for Integrative Genomics, Princeton University
Code, simulations and the source of this page: github.com/AkeyLab/pgsbridge

Polygenic scores lose accuracy when they are carried across populations. The question this study asks is which part of that loss is unavoidable and which part is recoverable through better data and better methods. It answers the question by removing every practical difficulty at once. Suppose every causal variant and every effect size were known exactly, were the same in both populations, and there were no linkage disequilibrium. What transferability would remain?

The answer is that a population has a ceiling on how well any genotype-based predictor can do, and that ceiling is a property of the population rather than of the predictor. Transferability is the ratio of two such ceilings.

Both of those statements can be made exact, and the two expressions below are the whole of the theory. The first says that a population's ceiling is fixed by just three quantities: the additive genetic variance , meaning how much of the trait is heritable; the average self-kinship , which summarises relatedness and structure within the sample; and the non-genetic variance , which is everything about the trait that no genotype can reach. The second says that transferability from a discovery population A to a target population B is the target's ceiling divided by the discovery population's. Section 1 takes the first expression apart and section 2 the second, so nothing here has to be absorbed at once.

Written PA for prediction accuracy and T for transferability. Under Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium the average self-kinship is exactly one half, and the first expression reduces to the familiar narrow-sense heritability.

01

The ceiling in one population

Give a predictor perfect knowledge: every causal variant, every effect size, no estimation error at all. It still cannot predict the part of the trait that is not genetic. What is left is a ceiling, and it belongs to the population.

The ceiling for this population, evaluated at the values in the panel. Every number moves as you drag.

The phenotypic variance of one individual, split in two

Prediction accuracy is the genetic share of the bar. It is the fraction of phenotypic variance a perfect genotype-based predictor removes, and nothing can remove more. The whole bar is the baseline: the error left by a predictor that ignores genotype entirely and gives every individual the population mean. That is the comparison the manuscript measures accuracy against, and its expected loss is exactly the total phenotypic variance.

The special case that makes the rest of the paper simple

Self-kinship is the probability that the two alleles an individual carries at a site are identical by descent. Under Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium every individual has self-kinship exactly one half, so the average is one half too, and the ceiling collapses to the narrow-sense heritability.

Why this matters

Nothing in this expression mentions a prediction algorithm. Two populations can differ in how predictable a trait is even when the trait has exactly the same genetic architecture in both, because they differ in allele frequencies, in relatedness structure, and in environmental variation. That is the whole engine of what follows.

02

Transferability is a ratio of two ceilings

Now there are two populations, so from here on the quantities carry a subscript. The population you tuned in section 1 becomes the discovery population A, and a second one, B, is added as the target. Build the optimal predictor in A and apply it in B. Because the predictor is already perfect, the only thing that changes is which population you are measuring in. Transferability is therefore the target's ceiling divided by the discovery population's ceiling, and there is no reason for that ratio to be at most one. Under Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium both ceilings are heritabilities, so transferability is exactly .

Reading the signs

Raising the additive genetic variance or the average self-kinship in the target population raises transferability, because both raise how much of the target's trait variance is genetic. Raising the non-genetic variance in the target lowers it. Doing any of the three in the discovery population does the opposite, and the curves cross at exactly one wherever the two populations agree.

Why the baseline predictor uses the true mean, not the sample mean

These simulated individuals are related to one another, so the sample mean of the trait carries a term in the average off-diagonal kinship that the closed form, which concerns a single individual's variance, does not. Comparing against the true population mean removes that mismatch, and averaging the numerator and denominator losses over 50 redraws before dividing removes the variance a ratio of two noisy estimates would otherwise carry. In the coalescent simulations of sections 6 to 8 the individuals are unrelated, the off-diagonal term is zero, and the ordinary sample estimator is used unchanged.

Out of Africa, and a reversed direction

Now let demographic history set the allele frequencies rather than specifying them. African, European and East Asian samples were simulated under an Out-of-Africa coalescent model. What empirical studies report

Polygenic scores trained in African-ancestry cohorts transfer comparatively well into non-African populations, and scores trained in European cohorts lose accuracy when applied to African-ancestry individuals. The direction is the opposite of the one above.

The two are not in conflict

They answer different questions. An empirical transferability is shaped at once by linkage disequilibrium, incomplete ascertainment of causal variants, finite training samples, estimation error and possible effect-size heterogeneity. This model removes all of them by construction. So the reversal is informative rather than contradictory: allele-frequency differences on their own favour transfer into African populations, which means the factors left out of the model must not only dominate the empirical pattern but be strong enough to turn it around.

Why the model produces this without any tagging argument

Rare variants are usually said to hurt portability because linkage disequilibrium tags them poorly and because selection can make their effects population-specific. Neither mechanism exists here: every causal variant is known and every effect size is shared. The widening comes from one thing only. A variant that is rare in Europeans is often absent outright from the European sample and still segregating in the African one, so it contributes additive genetic variance in one population and none in the other.

09

What the model deliberately removes

Every assumption here was made on purpose, and each one costs something. The point of the idealization is not that it is realistic. It is that removing a difficulty is the only way to find out how much of the outcome that difficulty was responsible for.

Linkage disequilibrium is absent removes imperfect tagging

Causal variants are assumed uncorrelated with one another, and the predictor is built on the causal variants themselves rather than on markers that tag them.

Why remove it. In a real polygenic score, a marker that tags a causal variant well in the discovery population may tag it poorly elsewhere, and that alone reduces portability. Leaving linkage disequilibrium in makes it impossible to say whether an observed loss came from population genetics or from tagging.

What it costs. The framework cannot speak to how much of a real portability gap is a tagging problem. It can only say that whatever remains after tagging is fixed is bounded by the ceiling computed here.

Effect sizes are identical across populations removes heterogeneity

The same causal variants carry the same effects in every population. Only allele frequencies, relatedness structure and non-genetic variance differ.

Why remove it. This is the assumption that isolates population genetics. If effects were allowed to differ, any difference in prediction accuracy could be attributed to the effects rather than to the populations, and the question being asked would disappear.

What it costs. Real effects may genuinely differ through gene-by-environment interaction or local adaptation. The manuscript names extending the framework to population-specific effects as the most important next step.

Every causal variant is known, and none is missed removes ascertainment

The predictor is the true genetic value, not an estimate of it. There is no discovery step, no significance threshold and no variant selection.

Why remove it. Incomplete ascertainment is itself population-dependent: a variant common enough to be discovered in one population may be too rare to be discovered in another. That interacts with allele frequency in exactly the place this study wants to look.

What it costs. Real scores are built from an ascertained subset, so the ceiling here is not attainable in practice. That is the intended reading: it is a bound, and the distance to it is the quantity of interest.

The discovery sample is effectively infinite removes estimation error

Because the predictor is specified rather than fitted, it carries no sampling variability, no shrinkage, no hyperparameter tuning and no winner's curse.

Why remove it. Finite discovery samples are the single largest practical driver of poor portability, and they are also the one most likely to improve. Separating them out is what makes the intrinsic and recoverable split in section 10 meaningful.

What it costs. None of the results here describe how quickly a real score approaches the ceiling as sample size grows. They describe only where the ceiling is.

Only additive effects exist removes interaction

The trait follows Fisher's polygenic model with additive genetic effects, an uncorrelated non-genetic component of constant variance, and no interaction of any kind.

Why remove it. Additivity is what makes the optimal predictor the conditional expectation and gives the ceiling a closed form in three quantities.

What it costs. Gene-by-gene and gene-by-environment interaction are excluded, and the manuscript lists both as directions for a more complete account of the limits of polygenic prediction.

10

Unavoidable against recoverable

This last panel is not a result of the paper. It is the Discussion's closing proposal made arithmetic, so that the idea can be handled rather than only read.

Read this before the numbers

The manuscript proposes that a cross-population prediction gap separates into an intrinsic component, arising from population differences in genetic predictability, and a recoverable component, arising from imperfect data and methods. It proposes the split; it does not derive or validate a formula for it. The arithmetic below is the natural way to carry out that split given that transferability is a ratio, and it is offered as an illustration of the proposal, not as a validated estimator. Treat every number it produces as a way of organising a comparison rather than as a measurement.

Transferability is a ratio, so the split is multiplicative, and additive on the logarithmic scale.
